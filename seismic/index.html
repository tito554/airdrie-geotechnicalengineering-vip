Seismic site assessment in Airdrie addresses the evaluation of local ground response under earthquake loading, a critical consideration given Alberta's documented seismic hazard, particularly from distant Cordilleran events and potential induced seismicity. The near-surface geology of Airdrie, characterized by glacial till, glaciofluvial sands, and silt overlying Cretaceous bedrock, directly influences site amplification potential. A comprehensive investigation defines the dynamic properties of these soils, ensuring compliance with the National Building Code of Canada (NBC) and the structural design requirements outlined in CSA S6 for bridges and CSA A23.3 for concrete structures. This process begins with a targeted geotechnical investigation and often relies on Standard Penetration Test (SPT) data to estimate liquefaction susceptibility and soil stiffness profiles.

Canadian geotechnical practice for seismic studies follows rigorous standardized methods to quantify soil behavior. The Standard Penetration Test (SPT) provides disturbed samples and N-values corrected for energy and overburden, which are essential inputs for liquefaction triggering analyses per Idriss and Boulanger methodologies. Undisturbed sampling and advanced laboratory testing are required to measure shear wave velocity (Vs) or cyclic resistance directly. Accurate soil classification per the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS) is fundamental, as the seismic response of fine-grained soils is governed by plasticity. Therefore, determining Atterberg limits and performing grain size analysis by sieve and hydrometer are mandatory to distinguish between susceptible silts and non-susceptible clays, directly impacting the assessment of cyclic softening.

In Airdrie, seismic considerations are integrated into a variety of projects beyond high-rise towers. Critical infrastructure such as schools, emergency response facilities, and bridge foundations require site-specific seismic hazard analysis. Commercial and industrial developments in the city's expanding corridors often involve flexible structures where seismic-induced settlement, rather than structural failure, governs the design. A detailed soil mechanics study evaluates post-liquefaction volumetric strain and potential bearing capacity loss. For engineered fills supporting these structures, the Proctor test (Standard or Modified) ensures that compaction meets specifications, minimizing the risk of densification and excessive settlement during a seismic event.

The deliverable is a comprehensive seismic design report providing Site Class determination per NBC Table 4.1.8.4.A, liquefaction potential maps, and recommendations for foundation design or ground improvement. The process moves from targeted drilling and geophysical surveys to advanced cyclic laboratory testing, culminating in numerical analysis. This data-driven approach provides Airdrie developers and structural engineers with the essential parameters—from peak ground acceleration (PGA) to spring constants—required for a resilient and code-compliant design, effectively mitigating the financial and safety risks associated with seismic ground failure.
